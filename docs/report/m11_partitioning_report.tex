\section{Scope}

FESOM2 reads its domain decomposition from files (\texttt{dist\_N}), so a better partition is a
drop-in change: no model code, no recompilation, no effect on any other configuration. This
campaign asked what that degree of freedom is worth for the C++/Kokkos port, on both backends,
and whether the node \emph{numbering} of the mesh (which fixes memory-access locality) is worth
changing as well.

The short answer: on GPU the shipped partitions leave 8--20\,\% of the model step on the table,
and the single most valuable knob (\minconn) has never been active in any FESOM partition ever
generated. On CPU the gains are smaller (4--8\,\%) and come from a different place --- external
partitioning engines and load-balance slack, not \minconn. The two backends want opposite
things from a partition, and one recipe does not serve both.

The campaign also produced a result we did not go looking for: \textbf{repartitioning these
meshes occasionally produces partitions on which the model diverges}, with any recipe ---
including the stock one --- and no offline metric predicts which. Eight such partitions were
caught. This turns a stability screen from a formality into the load-bearing part of any
adoption procedure, and it is why every number below is labelled with its evidence.

Meshes: CORE2 (127k surface nodes), fArc (638k), DARS (3.16M), NG5 (7.40M). All runs are cold
starts at each mesh's stability-ladder time step (CORE2 1800\,s, fArc 900\,s, DARS 120\,s, NG5
180\,s), JRA55 1958 forcing, PHC initial state, frozen certified binaries.

\section{What was tried}

\textbf{METIS options.} FESOM calls \texttt{METIS\_PartGraphRecursive}, which silently accepts
and ignores three options: the communication-volume objective (\texttt{OBJTYPE=VOL}), contiguity
(\contig) and \minconn\ (minimise the maximum number of neighbouring subdomains). We switched
the call to \texttt{PartGraphKway} and swept: the objective, \minconn, \contig, load-balance
slack (\texttt{UFACTOR} 10/30/100), scalar vertical weights $w = a + \mathrm{nlev}$ for
$a \in \{0, 15, 40, 100\}$, hierarchical two-level partitions, and METIS seeds. To be fair to
the original authors: the Recursive choice is documented in \texttt{fort\_part.c} with a
measured justification on one mesh; what has changed since is the hardware.

\textbf{External engines.} KaHIP, KaMinPar and Mt-KaHyPar, each fed the same node-weighted dual
graph, under the same scalar weight sweep.

\textbf{Node renumbering.} Hilbert space-filling-curve reordering of the mesh files themselves
(a deeper change than a \texttt{dist\_N} swap: every reference run pinned to the old numbering
must be re-baselined), plus reverse Cuthill--McKee.

\textbf{Cross-architecture check.} The winning GPU settings were re-raced on DKRZ's GH200
partition (dolpung), whose fabric has no GPUDirect and stages every halo through pinned host
memory --- a deliberately different per-message cost structure.

\section{How things were measured}

Speed numbers come from \emph{races}: all arms in one SLURM allocation, interleaved repetitions,
300 steps, min-of-$N$ against the shipped partition of the same mesh, at the ladder time step.
The five mesh-definition files are md5-verified identical across arms before any leg runs, so
arms differ only in the partition. Offline partition metrics (edge cut, halo volume, partner
counts, imbalance) were computed for every candidate --- and used only for design, never as a
gate (Section~5).

A raced gain is not a result until it passes two gates:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Stability screen: 3{,}000 steps at the target rank count}, return code and
trajectory checked. This bar exists because a 150-step winner on DARS
(\minconn, $-4.5\,\%$) diverged between step 150 and step 3{,}000 while its baseline ran clean.
\item \textbf{Accuracy gate: the control envelope.} Twenty steps with per-step output; the arm's
temperature, salinity and SSH rms differences against base must sit inside the spread of at
least three \emph{seed controls} --- re-rolls of the shipped recipe differing only in the METIS
seed, each verified to be a genuinely different partition. Any partition change perturbs the
state at round-off and the differences grow chaotically at fronts, so ``zero difference'' is not
available; ``indistinguishable from an ordinary repartitioning'' is the operative standard. The
rms-inside-the-envelope yardstick is fixed campaign policy; quantiles are reported as context
but never decide.
\end{itemize}

Certified partitions leave the campaign only through a promotion tool that refuses any
\texttt{dist\_N} lacking a recorded 3{,}000-step clean run at exactly that rank count.

Two regularities. First, \textbf{every gain re-measured at 3{,}000 steps came back equal to or
larger than its 300-step race value} (eight matched pairs; e.g.\ DARS GPU $-18.6 \to -19.7\,\%$,
CORE2 GPU $-7.5 \to -8.1\,\%$): the short race systematically under-reports, so these numbers
are conservative. Second, the backend split is consistent everywhere: the two knobs
(\contig{}+slack) that gain five points on DARS GPU \emph{cost} a point on DARS CPU.

\subsection{Why: the GPU pays per message, the CPU pays for owned work}

Across all nine raced groups, the number of communication partners is the only offline metric
family that correlates positively with GPU step time --- every volume-like metric (edge cut,
halo bytes, replicated elements) correlates \emph{negatively}: arms that ship more data are
faster when they talk to fewer neighbours. \minconn\ is the only METIS objective that targets
partner count, which is why it wins on GPU and why the win was invisible for as long as the
Recursive call ignored it.

Across meshes, the size of the GPU gain tracks the \emph{fractional reduction in the maximum
partner count} (Table~2) --- the max, not the mean, because the step waits for the slowest rank.
By mean partner count the three points are indistinguishable.

On CPU no offline metric orders the arms --- not imbalance, not edge cut, not partner count
(median within-group Spearman $\rho$ straddles zero for every column). The often-quoted
imbalance correlation ($r=0.91$) is a statement about where time goes \emph{within} one
partition, not about which partition is faster. On CPU you have to race. What does generalise is
that the external engines win at every large-CPU point, and are simultaneously the
best-behaved arms in the accuracy gates.

\subsection{Node renumbering: CORE2 only}

Hilbert renumbering is worth $-1.2$ to $-2.4\,\%$ (CPU) and $-5.0\,\%$ (GPU, one node) on CORE2
and is $\sim$94\,\% additive with repartitioning ($-5.8\,\%$ combined at 512 ranks). It is worth
nothing on any other mesh: fArc, DARS and NG5 already ship at 88\,\% element-gather locality
against CORE2's 27.6\,\%. Because renumbering rewrites the mesh files --- forcing every pinned
reference run to be re-baselined --- we recommend it only where that disruption is priced in;
the repartitioning gain is a pure \texttt{dist\_N} swap and carries no such cost.

\subsection{Accuracy: the control envelope did real work}

The gates are a comparison against ordinary repartitionings, and they discriminated in both
directions. At CORE2\,4\,GPU the winners sit \emph{below} every control; at DARS\,2048\,CPU
KaMinPar sits below every control on temperature and salinity. Conversely fArc\,16\,GPU failed
(both arms above a four-control temperature envelope) and CORE2\,864 failed on salt against
five controls --- both points are speed wins that we do not recommend. Control spreads vary by
an order of magnitude between meshes ($\times$1.05 on DARS, $\times$3.9 on CORE2 at 864), so a
fixed rms threshold would be meaningless; the per-mesh envelope is what makes the verdicts
comparable.

One mechanism deserves its own sentence: partitions that make the SSH solver converge
\emph{faster} systematically show larger SSH differences, because the solver stops on a relative
residual --- stopping sooner lands further from the reference. This inflates the SSH statistic
for exactly the partitions one wants; it is visible at CORE2 and DARS and is part of why the
DARS GPU gate is being decided on a widened envelope rather than a first pass.

\subsection{Cross-architecture: the lever is real off-A100, but only proven at one node}

On the GH200 testbed the CORE2 single-node result reproduces and slightly exceeds A100
(\minconn\ $-8.9$ vs $-8.1\,\%$; two independent jobs within 0.6\,pp). A pre-registered
prediction that DARS\,64 (16 GH200 nodes) would beat its A100 $-18.6\,\%$ \textbf{failed}: the
measured effect is $-1$ to $-3\,\%$, and fArc (4 nodes) is a clean null at min-of-5. Once the
halo leaves pinned host on the inter-node fabric, the per-message premium largely vanishes on
that machine. Conclusions: the lever is not an A100 artefact (different silicon, different
transport, same win at one node), but multi-node gains must be measured per fabric, never
extrapolated --- and min-of-$N$ is a biased estimator whenever the baseline is noisier than the
arms (on dolpung it always was; medians must be reported alongside).

\section{What we learned beyond the numbers}

\textbf{1. Repartitioning occasionally produces fatal partitions, with any recipe.} Eight
partitions caught so far: \minconn-family and slack-only arms on NG5 and DARS at both 64 and
2048 ranks --- and one \emph{seed-only re-roll of the stock shipped recipe}, which blew up at
step 14. The failure is always local (one spot runs away while global diagnostics stay sane for
tens of steps) and has no offline signature: the dying partitions score \emph{better} than
survivors on every scorecard column we computed. The shipped partitions in production are, in
this precise sense, lucky seeds.

\textbf{2. The remedy is cheap and verified: re-roll the seed.} On both meshes where a winner
died, regenerating the identical arm with a different METIS seed produced a partition that
passed the full screen (NG5, DARS). A failure is a lottery ticket, not a verdict on the knobs.

\textbf{3. Short tests prove nothing about stability.} A 5-step smoke passes on a partition that
dies at step 71; a 150-step race passes on one that dies before step 3{,}000. Screen length is
part of any claim's identity, and the 3{,}000-step floor caught what would otherwise have been
shipped twice.

\textbf{4. Accuracy needs an envelope, not a threshold or a single control.} One control
condemned an innocent arm (its class spans $\times$2.7--3.9 between seeds); a control that
coincides with the base partition measures round-off and condemns everything. At least three
controls, each demonstrated distinct, before any verdict.

\textbf{5. Protocol discipline is what the numbers rest on.} Cold starts must run at the
ladder time step (four separate incidents of racing at production $\Delta t$ cost three findings
and, once, the baseline itself); \texttt{srun: task Killed} is teardown noise --- the real error
is hundreds of lines earlier; and a pre-registered prediction that fails (Section 4.5) is worth
more than a post-hoc explanation that fits.

\section{Recommendations}

\textbf{Upstream (FESOM2), in order of value:}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item Switch \texttt{fort\_part.c} to \texttt{METIS\_PartGraphKway} so \minconn, \contig\ and
\texttt{OBJTYPE=VOL} actually reach METIS, and expose the partitioner options as runtime
switches rather than recompilation.
\item Ship the adoption procedure with it: \textbf{every newly generated partition --- any
engine, any options, any seed --- must run $\geq$3{,}000 steps at the target rank count at the
mesh's cold-start time step before use; on failure, re-roll the seed and screen again.} This is
not a tax on the new options: the stock recipe fails it too.
\item The isolated-node repair in \texttt{check\_partitioning} currently seeds its candidate
list with a single neighbour; build the full list. Update to METIS 5.2.1.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Per-machine settings measured here} (Levante): GPU runs --- \minconn\ on, the further
knobs (\contig, slack) decided per point by a race, since they gain five points on DARS and cost
elsewhere; CPU runs --- an external engine (KaMinPar/Mt-KaHyPar) with $w = 100 + \mathrm{nlev}$
where available, else \texttt{UFACTOR=30} slack; Hilbert renumbering on CORE2-class meshes only,
and only where re-baselining is acceptable.

\textbf{What not to do:} do not gate a partition on the offline scorecard (it ranks dying
partitions above surviving ones); do not certify with a smoke test; do not transfer multi-node
GPU gains across fabrics without measuring.

\section{Status and open items}

The DARS\,64 and NG5\,64 GPU accuracy gates are in the queue at the time of writing; DARS's
first pass sits just outside a three-control envelope on all fields and is being re-judged
against five controls, with the SSH-stopping mechanism (Section 4.4) as recorded context. If the
$-19.7\,\%$ arm remains out of class, the fallback positions are explicit: \minconn\ alone
($-14.3\,\%$, near-in-class on first pass), or adoption as a documented exception --- the gate
verdict stays as measured either way. GH200 multi-node behaviour, and the question of whether a
fabric with GPUDirect (JUPITER) prices messages like the A100 or like dolpung, are open and
should be answered by racing, not by analogy.
